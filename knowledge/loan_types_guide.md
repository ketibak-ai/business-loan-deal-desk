# Business Loan Types Guide

## Term Loans
A term loan is a lump sum repaid on a fixed schedule, usually monthly. Banks use them for expansion, acquisitions, equipment and refinancing. Terms usually run 3 to 7 years. The payment schedule (amortization) can be longer than the term, which leaves a balloon payment due at maturity. Rates are fixed or floating over SOFR. Banks usually want two or more years in business, a debt service coverage ratio (DSCR) of 1.25x or better, and collateral or a personal guarantee.

## Lines of Credit
A line of credit (revolver) lets a business draw, repay and draw again up to a limit. Interest is charged only on the drawn balance. The unused part carries an unused commitment fee, often 15 to 50 bps a year. Lines usually last 1 to 2 years and renew annually. They are secured by receivables and inventory through a blanket lien, and often have a borrowing base that limits draws to a share of eligible receivables. Ask only for the line size you need, because unused capacity costs you fees and costs the bank capital.

## Equipment Loans
An equipment loan finances a machine, vehicle or technology. The equipment is the collateral. Lenders advance 80 to 100% of cost, and the term usually matches the equipment's useful life, often 3 to 7 years. Because the asset secures the loan, equipment loans are easier to get than unsecured loans. Leasing is an alternative when you want lower payments or expect to upgrade.

## Commercial Real Estate Loans
A commercial real estate (CRE) mortgage finances owner-occupied or investment property. Typical structures have a 5 to 10 year term on a 20 to 25 year amortization, so a large balloon is due at maturity and must be refinanced or repaid. Lenders usually cap the loan at 70 to 80% of appraised value (loan-to-value) and want DSCR of 1.20x to 1.35x. Fixed rates are often priced over the Treasury or swap curve and may carry a prepayment penalty.

## Invoice and Receivables Financing
Invoice financing advances cash against unpaid customer invoices, usually about 80% of eligible receivables. It suits businesses that sell to other businesses on payment terms. It grows with sales and is fast to set up, but it costs more than a bank line. Factoring is a variation in which the lender buys the invoices and often collects from your customers directly.

## Merchant Cash Advances
A merchant cash advance (MCA) is not a loan. A funder buys a share of future sales for a lump sum and collects a fixed daily or weekly amount, or a percentage of card sales. Pricing is a factor rate such as 1.35, meaning $1.35 repaid per $1 received. Because repayment is daily and short, a 1.35 factor over nine months works out to an APR near or above 80% after fees. Treat an MCA as a last resort, and compare its APR against a bank or SBA loan before signing.

## Choosing the Right Loan
Match the loan to its use. Use a line of credit for short-term working capital needs such as inventory and receivables. Use a term loan for long-lived investments. Use equipment financing for machinery and a CRE mortgage for property. Using short-term money for long-term assets squeezes cash flow. Using long-term debt for working capital costs more than it needs to.
