# SBA Loan Programs

## SBA 7(a) Basics
The SBA 7(a) program is the SBA's main small business loan. A bank or other approved lender makes the loan, and the SBA guarantees part of it: typically 85% for loans of $150,000 or less and 75% for larger loans. The maximum loan amount is $5 million. Uses include working capital, equipment, real estate, refinancing and buying a business. Terms can run up to 10 years for working capital and equipment and up to 25 years for real estate. Program limits, fees and rules change, so check sba.gov for the current rules.

## SBA 7(a) Rates and Fees
SBA caps how far above the base rate a 7(a) lender can price, by loan size: base + 6.5% for loans of $50,000 or less, + 6.0% up to $250,000, + 4.5% up to $350,000, and + 3.0% above $350,000. Prime is the usual base; since March 1, 2026 lenders may also use SOFR or the 5- or 10-year Treasury for variable loans. Borrowers pay an upfront guarantee fee on the guaranteed portion. For loans approved from October 1, 2025 to September 30, 2026 (FY2026) it is 2% for loans of $150,000 or less, 3% up to $700,000, and above that 3.5% of the first $1 million guaranteed plus 3.75% of the rest. FY2026 waives the fee for manufacturers on loans up to $950,000. The schedule changes every fiscal year, so check sba.gov. Compare offers using APR, which includes these fees.

## Why Banks Use the SBA Guarantee
The guarantee transfers most of the credit loss to the government. The guaranteed share needs very little capital and carries little expected loss, so lenders can approve borrowers with short histories, limited collateral or thin cash flow. If a conventional loan is declined because of collateral shortfall or fewer than two years in business, ask whether the lender would do it as an SBA 7(a) loan.

## SBA 504 Loans
The SBA 504 program finances major fixed assets such as owner-occupied real estate and long-lived equipment. A typical structure is 50% from a bank first mortgage, 40% from a Certified Development Company (CDC) second loan backed by the SBA, and 10% from the borrower. The CDC portion carries a long fixed rate, often 20 or 25 years. The low down payment preserves working capital. The business generally must occupy most of the property.

## SBA Eligibility
To qualify, a business generally must be for-profit, operate in the United States, meet SBA size standards and show that it cannot get credit on reasonable terms elsewhere. Owners of 20% or more usually give personal guarantees. Lenders still underwrite repayment ability, typically looking for DSCR of about 1.15x or more and acceptable owner credit.

## SBA Credit Score Requirements
SBA lenders review the personal credit of every owner of 20% or more, because those owners usually guarantee the loan, along with business credit and cash flow. Until March 1, 2026, 7(a) small loans of $350,000 or less had to pass a FICO Small Business Scoring Service (SBSS) prescreen with a minimum score of 165. Since March 1, 2026, the SBA no longer requires the SBSS score; lenders use generally accepted credit analysis, which can include their own scoring models or SBSS. Many SBA lenders still look for owner personal scores of roughly 650 to 680 or higher, but requirements vary by lender and strong cash flow can offset a weaker score.

## SBA Prepayment Fee
The SBA prepayment penalty, officially a subsidy recoupment fee, applies when you pay off an SBA loan early. SBA 7(a) loans with terms of 15 years or longer carry it if you voluntarily prepay 25% or more of the balance in the first three years: 5% of the amount prepaid in year 1, 3% in year 2 and 1% in year 3. Loans under 15 years have no SBA prepayment fee. SBA sets this fee, so the bank cannot waive it; plan any refinance for after year 3.

## Why SBA Rates Sit Near the Cap
Most SBA lenders sell the guaranteed portion of a 7(a) loan to investors at a premium and keep a servicing fee of at least 1% a year on the sold part. The premium grows with the loan's rate, so lowering your rate also lowers the bank's sale income. That is why SBA loans are usually priced near the SBA maximum and bankers have little room to discount the rate. Negotiate fees, the guarantee and collateral instead, or compare a conventional loan.

## Graduating from SBA to a Conventional Loan
As a business grows it can refinance an SBA loan into a conventional small-business or middle-market loan. Lenders typically look for three or more years in business, owner credit of 700 or more, debt service coverage of about 1.35x or better, debt to EBITDA of 3x or less, collateral or strong cash flow, and ideally EBITDA of $1 million or more. Graduating removes the guarantee fee and SBA rules and often lowers the rate, but conventional loans usually have shorter amortization, which can raise the payment, and heavier covenants. Watch the SBA prepayment fee on 15-year-plus loans in the first three years.
